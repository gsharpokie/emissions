Who Emits the Carbon?

Which countries put the most carbon dioxide into the air? Does the answer change if you count per person, or add up history? And can a country’s economy grow while its emissions shrink? Make nine guesses, then see what the data show.

The fuller picture

Three ways to count, three different rankings. (Switch the chart above between views to see them.) China emits the most each year. Americans emit the most per person among large countries. And the U.S. has emitted the most in total since 1750. Each way of counting answers a different question: who emits now, whose lifestyles emit the most, and who did the most to put the CO₂ that’s already in the air there. Debates over who should cut how much often come down to which count people think matters most.

How can emissions fall while an economy grows? Common explanations include changing how electricity is made (less coal; more natural gas, renewables, or nuclear), using energy more efficiently, and shifting toward service industries that use less energy than heavy manufacturing. Some of the drop came from moving factories overseas, which is why the consumption-based count matters. But in the 22 countries listed below, emissions fell even when imports are counted.

The 22 countries. These countries of more than a million people cut both their production-based and their consumption-based CO₂ by at least 5% from 2005 to 2022 while their economies grew (after inflation): Austria, Bulgaria, Croatia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Spain, Sweden, Trinidad and Tobago, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Several more, including Canada and New Zealand, came close.

Progress and how far there is to go. Fossil emissions worldwide grew about 20% from 2005 to 2015, and about 10% from 2015 to 2025. The Global Carbon Project projects a small drop of about 0.4% in 2026, partly from more solar and wind power and partly from an oil shock tied to the Iran conflict. It estimates that solar, wind, and electric vehicles now offset about two-thirds of the emissions that rising energy demand would otherwise add. But its researchers warn the shift isn’t yet fast enough for a lasting decline, and once deforestation and other land-use emissions are added, total CO₂ emissions are projected to hit a record in 2026.

A very unequal footprint. The world’s low-income countries are home to about 9% of the world’s people but produced about half of 1% of its fossil CO₂ in 2025. The average person in those countries emitted about 0.3 metric tons, compared with 14.5 for the average American.

Other fields to bring in. Economists study whether countries tend to pollute more as they industrialize and then less as they get richer, an idea called the environmental Kuznets curve, and how to price carbon. Political scientists study why cooperation on a shared problem is hard when the costs of acting fall on each country but the benefits are shared by all. Geographers and climate scientists study where the effects of warming fall, often on places that emitted little.

Talk about it

  1. Which guess was furthest off? Did you expect more or less progress than the data show?
  2. Should a country be judged by its total emissions, its emissions per person, its emissions over history, or the emissions from the goods its people buy? Who benefits from each way of counting?
  3. If a country’s emissions fall because it moved factories overseas, has it made progress? What would you need to know to decide?
  4. China’s emissions per person are higher than the EU’s but far lower than the U.S.’s. How should that shape what each is expected to do?
  5. Global emissions are still near record highs even though more than 20 countries have cut theirs. What would it take for the world total to fall?

About the data

Emissions are carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels and from industry (mainly cement), not other greenhouse gases or deforestation, from the Global Carbon Budget 2026 as compiled by Our World in Data (downloaded November 14, 2026). Most figures count emissions where they’re produced. Consumption-based figures (questions 7 and 8) instead count emissions from producing the goods and services a country’s people use, wherever they were made; those are available from 1990 to 2024. Economic growth uses GDP adjusted for inflation and living costs from the Maddison Project Database, which ends in 2022, so the comparisons of emissions and growth run from 2005 to 2022. The Global Carbon Project’s own headline totals count slightly differently from this dataset, so news reports may give slightly different global figures.
